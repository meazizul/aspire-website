Why ASPIRE

Real slides. Real time. Real understanding.

Within seconds of live

Median listener delay is 4 seconds, measured with real listeners connected. Not a transcript after the talk.

Every new slide, described once

Slide changes are detected automatically. Build-up animations and repeated slides are recognised, so nothing is read twice.

Never talks over the speaker

Descriptions are placed in the presenter's natural pauses. The held speech is returned faster than real time with its pitch intact.

Nothing to install for listeners

Listeners open a link on any phone or laptop. The page works with screen readers and starts with a single keystroke.

Runs on the presenter's Mac

Works with any slide software and any meeting tool, because it reads the screen and system audio directly.

Peer-reviewed and field-tested

Three published studies with blind and low-vision participants, plus a live deployment at an international conference.

The problem we solve

Slides carry the argument. Blind listeners get the voice-over.

In lectures, conferences and webinars, the chart, the diagram and the bullet list are where the meaning lives. Presenters rarely describe them. Human describers are scarce and expensive, and recorded descriptions arrive days later. ASPIRE closes that gap while the talk is still happening.

How it works

A five-stage pipeline on a single laptop

ASPIRE runs on the presenter's Mac. It captures the screen and the system audio, decides when a new slide has appeared, writes a one-sentence description, turns it into speech, and mixes it into the live audio that listeners receive.

Capture

Screen video and system audio, straight from macOS. No virtual audio drivers, no plug-ins.

ScreenCaptureKit · ffmpeg

Detect the slide change

Perceptual hashing spots a visual change; a short stability window lets build-ups finish; text and description matching stop repeats.

pHash · OCR-text dedup

Read and describe

On-device OCR extracts the text; a vision-language model writes a short spoken-form description of what matters on the slide.

Apple Vision OCR · Claude Haiku 4.5

Speak

A local neural voice renders the description in under a second, with numbers normalised so they are read correctly.

Kokoro TTS, on device

Mix and stream

A 50 fps mixer places the description in the next natural pause and streams the result to listeners' phones.

WebRTC · HLS fallback
The core idea

Borrow a few seconds, give them straight back

0.0 s · level with live

Animation runs at 2× speed. Timing comes from the same audio sample you can play below.

presenter's speech spoken slide description held speech, returned at up to 1.5×
1 · Pass-throughNo standing delay. Audio goes straight to listeners until a slide changes.
2 · Describe in a pauseThe description waits for a pause of 0.3 s or more, then plays. The presenter's speech is held meanwhile.
3 · Reclaim the timeHeld speech is returned with pitch-preserving time compression, 1.0× up to 1.5× depending on how far behind the listener is.
4 · Level againOnce the backlog is gone the audio is untouched again. Nothing is cut, nothing is lost.

How we got here

From a Wizard-of-Oz study to a live system in eighteen months

  1. 2025

    The idea is validated by hand

    A Wizard-of-Oz study with four blind and low-vision participants: talks with human-edited slide descriptions. Listeners wanted short, factual descriptions placed in the pauses, not over the speaker. Presented as a poster at ASSETS 2025.

  2. Early 2026

    The pipeline is automated

    Slide detection, AI description and speech synthesis run end to end on recorded talks. Twelve participants, comprehension from 50.0% to 88.9%. Published at DIS 2026 with a Best Paper Honorable Mention.

  3. May 2026

    It goes live

    First real-time version: native macOS capture, Claude Haiku vision, an on-device voice, and WebRTC streaming to phones. The reference implementation is released as open source.

  4. July 2026

    Latency drops from 30 seconds to 4

    An instrumented measurement campaign finds the real bottleneck in the audio pipeline, not the AI model. Pitch-preserving catch-up, WebRTC-by-default and deadline pacing bring the median delay to 4 s with zero dropouts. The system is then deployed in a live field study at an international HCI conference with twelve blind and low-vision participants.

  5. October 2026

    Demonstrated at ASSETS 2026

    The real-time system is presented to the accessibility research community in Portugal. Pilot recall: 16.7% to 91.7%.

  6. Now

    From research system to product

    We are forming the company, lining up university and conference pilots, and raising a pre-seed round to build the one-click product and the integrations customers ask for.

Why now

Accessibility is becoming a legal deadline, and talks have moved online

Lectures, conferences and all-hands meetings are streamed by default now, and the obligation to make them accessible is shifting from goodwill to regulation. Live captions solved the audio side. Nobody has solved the slides.

2.2 billion

people worldwide live with a near or distance vision impairment.

World Health Organization
April 2026

US public universities and governments must meet WCAG 2.1 AA for web content under the ADA Title II rule, including live and recorded media; smaller entities follow in April 2027.

US Department of Justice
June 2025

The European Accessibility Act began to apply, extending accessibility duties to digital products and services across the EU.

European Commission

Universities and online education

Disability-services offices need a scalable way to make live lectures and recorded courses accessible. ASPIRE runs on the instructor's laptop and needs no change to how they teach.

Conferences and professional events

Organisers can offer every remote attendee a described stream with a link, instead of hiring describers for a handful of sessions.

Enterprise video platforms

The description engine fits naturally alongside live captions in meeting and webinar platforms, as an integration or a licensed component.
